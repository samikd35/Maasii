# Ethiopian table menu and feedback research

**Recommended direction.** Give every table a small branded card that opens the current menu, shows the venue’s accepted payment details, and makes it easy for guests to tell the kitchen what they thought of a particular dish. Give the operator one useful observation to act on each week. This is a concept for a pilot, based on desk research and the product direction agreed in chat. Assumptions are an Addis Ababa pilot with independent waiter-led venues, Amharic and English to start, and feedback shared privately with the venue.

The table card should feel like a good printed menu. The physical sign says “Tap or scan for the menu” and carries the same venue name and table number as the page. Use a warm paper background, high contrast text, one venue accent, a visible language switch, and prices lined up at the right. Keep one optional food photo above the fold at most and load other images after the text. Group dishes by the venue’s own categories, including a fasting category where appropriate. Provide a QR fallback on the same sign and keep a paper menu available for guests who need it.

| Direction | What the guest sees | What to test |
|---|---|---|
| Menu Board | A clear category list, price column and three fixed destinations for menu, feedback and payment options. Minimal images and no transition effects. | Establishes a low-friction baseline. |
| Table Card | A matched physical card and digital menu. The menu shows venue and table at the top, with a simple “I tried this” action on each dish detail. That action carries the dish name into a short feedback form. A visible button works without motion. | Recommended. Check whether guests understand the dish action and return to it after eating. |
| Kitchen Question | Add one optional, rotating question about a dish the kitchen is trying to improve. For example, “How was the portion of today’s mushroom tibs?” The regular review choices remain available. | Try after the standard form produces useful feedback. Keep answers separate from the standard ratings because a targeted prompt changes what gets reported. |

**The guest opens a menu.** The table’s NFC tag stores an HTTPS URL, and the camera QR leads to the same table page. The phone can open the link in its browser, with a notification or confirmation on some devices. The venue updates the menu and payment details on the server. Guests can browse the menu by category and open payment options or feedback whenever they choose. Verify link behavior on the actual guest phones used at pilot venues. A table URL provides context and can be shared or reopened. The system records self-selected dish feedback, with no claim of a verified order or current presence. [Android NFC documentation](https://developer.android.com/develop/connectivity/nfc/nfc), [Apple background tag reading](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corenfc/adding-support-for-background-tag-reading)

**Two review paths cover the visit and a specific dish.** The proposed interpretation of “two to four parts” is two review choices, with up to four optional topics in each. Keep a response short enough to complete from a phone with one hand. A guest can submit either review independently and skip topics and comments.

| Path | One required response | Up to four optional topics |
|---|---|---|
| This visit | “How was your visit?” Great / Okay / Needs work. | Food overall, service, wait time, paying. |
| A dish I tried | “Would you order it again?” Yes / Maybe / No. | Taste, portion, temperature, value. |

The dish page preselects the item and displays its name before submission. The feedback tab also lets someone look up an item they tried. Use concrete optional chips such as “Served cold” if that issue needs counting. A tap on the generic topic “Temperature” alone only records that the topic was selected. Let guests leave a note in their own language. Show “Shared privately with [venue]” above the submit button and “For help now, ask your server” where useful. A separate public review link is a possible future test. If a venue links Google reviews, show that option consistently regardless of private ratings, without incentives. [Google Maps review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en)

**Payment options work as a directory.** Each venue chooses the methods it currently accepts. A transfer entry shows the provider, recipient name, and a selectable account number with a copy button. A wallet entry can show a merchant code, copy control, provider instructions and an official static merchant QR supplied by the venue or its provider. Distinguish a bank transfer from a similarly named wallet. The amount comes from the existing bill. Guests complete payment in their own bank or wallet app, and venue staff use their usual confirmation process. The page label is “Payment options.”

An image of a QR on the paying phone is useful only when the provider supports importing it or another screen or printed sign is available. Test that flow rather than assuming gallery support. Offer the provider’s supported copyable code or transfer details when available, and keep the printed merchant QR at the cashier as a fallback. telebirr documents a merchant-code route alongside scanning. NBE’s ETHQR standard supports interoperability among participating providers. A venue with a working ETHQR can put it first and retain the alternatives it actually accepts. Use an owner-controlled edit screen, record payment-detail changes, and have the owner confirm the payee and QR before publishing. [telebirr payment instructions](https://www.ethiotelecom.et/telebirr/%E1%88%88%E1%8B%95%E1%89%83%E1%8B%8E%E1%89%BD-%E1%8A%A5%E1%8A%93-%E1%8A%A0%E1%8C%88%E1%88%8D%E1%8C%8E%E1%89%B6%E1%89%BD-%E1%8B%AD%E1%8A%AD%E1%8D%88%E1%88%89/), [NBE QR standard](https://nbe.gov.et/eipqrc/)

**The owner sees one change worth trying.** Start with a phone-friendly weekly view of counts by item and issue, distributions for the two questions, and the comments supporting each issue. An illustrative note using fictional data is “Mushroom tibs. Five of 16 dish responses said it was served cold. Check how long the dish waits between plating and serving, and look again next week.” Give the manager the ability to note what they changed. Comparisons should show counts, time windows, and what changed. Feedback is self-selected. Ratings describe respondents, while menu views describe browsing. Neither provides a sales denominator.

Use software to count and filter. Add AI once free-text volume justifies help reading it. Useful tasks are grouping Amharic, English, and mixed-language comments into familiar topics, drafting a short translation alongside the original, and suggesting a question for the kitchen. Test categorisation against venue-reviewed examples and show the underlying feedback for each summary. Keep the original wording and let managers correct labels. A useful precedent is Ovation’s menu item reporting. Its report associates overall feedback with items in POS orders, while the proposed form lets a guest select and rate a named dish directly. [Ovation item performance](https://intercom.help/ovation-bd4a0805515d/en/articles/15503353-menu-item-performance-report)

For an early pilot, keep feedback account-free and avoid asking for contact details. Limit access to venue staff and minimise table and timestamp data when sharing summaries. Free-text comments may still include personal data. Review local storage and transfer requirements before selecting an external AI provider or sending raw comments to it. [Ethiopian Personal Data Protection Proclamation, Article 22](https://justice.gov.et/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/seasiisia-seusiNse%C2%A1-1321-2016.pdf)

**A small pilot can separate the menu’s utility from the feedback’s value.** Start with one café and one casual restaurant that serve guests at the table. Observe the current menu and payment process, then put cards on a handful of tables for two weeks. Compare a QR-only sign with tap-plus-QR at comparable tables. Watch how quickly guests get to the menu, whether they can find and use payment details on one phone, and whether they submit a dish or visit response. Tag links by entry method if measuring tap versus scan. Treat page opens and copy actions as navigation signals. Observe the cashier’s process separately to assess payment friction. Ask each manager to pick one action from the weekly brief and decide whether it is worth paying for another month.

The smallest pilot product is the guest page, an owner screen to maintain menu items and accepted payment methods, and a private feedback list with counts. A setup fee can cover menu entry and signage, with a recurring fee per outlet for the live menu and weekly review. Pricing, review volume, payment details at each site, and willingness to pay remain open. For hotels, the same setup can serve one restaurant or lobby café as its own outlet.

## Market evidence

Desk research checked on 9 November 2026 in Addis Ababa time. Product descriptions are vendor claims. The public venue menu below is directly observable online. Table use, staff workflows and uptake remain to be verified in the field.

### Ethiopian products and substitutes

- **No’or** advertises NFC and QR access to menus and private feedback within an Ethiopian customer engagement and loyalty platform. Its café example puts the tap at the counter. The site leaves dish-level feedback and its availability outside the loyalty flow open. [No’or](https://noorconnects.com/website)
- **ZemTab** offers an Ethiopia-based live QR menu as a standalone option. Its broader restaurant and hotel ordering workflow can display payment QR codes and account numbers, with guests uploading or showing proof to staff. The payment display is described in the ordering workflow, while the standalone offer describes the menu. [ZemTab](https://www.zemtab.com/)
- **Addis QR Menu** advertises guest ratings, quick feedback, scan trends and popular-item insights. The indexed page leaves the basis for item popularity and the target of guest ratings open. **Bemi Creatives** advertises comments and ratings inside its Premium QR menu, with the rating target and visibility unspecified. Both descriptions came from indexed copies of first-party pages after fresh requests returned errors. [Addis QR Menu](https://addis-menu.com/), [Bemi Creatives](https://www.bemicreatives.com/)
- **TapServe** puts table QR ordering and broadly described customer feedback in its Pro POS tier. **MyOrder** uses table QR ordering to send tickets to the kitchen and directs guests to pay at the counter. **Cache**, an Addis-based hospitality operating system, describes an optional rating against the order after the guest uses its QR ordering flow. [TapServe](https://www.tapserve.et/), [MyOrder](https://myorder.et/), [Cache](https://cache.platform.et/)
- **Duka** offers a bilingual browser menu with table QR codes, item photos and dietary tags. **Get Menu** advertises converting photos of paper menus into editable QR menus for Ethiopian restaurants and hotels. **La Vie En Rose** in Addis Ababa links to a working Top Food App menu from its own venue site. Top Food App separately advertises private overall-visit feedback as an optional menu feature. [Duka](https://duka.et/), [Get Menu](https://getmenu.et/), [La Vie En Rose](https://lavieenroseaddis.com/), [Public venue menu](https://topfood.app/en/restaurants/500-la-vie-en-rose-cafe-restaurant/public), [Top Food feedback](https://ru.topfood.app/en/restaurant-feedback-form)
- **Public reviews** are a substitute for venue-wide feedback. Google Business Profile provides a review QR code, and Addis-focused Bota Review combines venue reviews with menus and prices. A2SV’s **RateEat** describes public dish-specific reviews and QR menus in its impact report. Its September 2026 Android listing focuses on Rwanda, so current Ethiopian coverage remains uncertain. [Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16816815), [Bota Review](https://botareview.et/), [A2SV impact report, Appendix XI](https://www.a2sv.org/pdfs/a2sv-5-years-of-impact.pdf), [RateEat Android listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.a2sv.rateeat_mobile)

### International precedents

- **TableQR** advertises private, account-free feedback linked to specific items from inside its view-only QR menu, alongside a separate public Google review link. Its public description leaves the mechanics of linking feedback to an order open. [Feedback feature](https://tableqr.co/features/customer-feedbacks/), [Menu demo FAQ](https://tableqr.co/qr-menu-demo/)
- **ThisDish** advertises a receipt QR that opens a browser survey for the specific dishes the guest ordered. The receipt ties feedback to a purchase. [ThisDish](https://thisdish.co/)
- **TapMe Contact** documents a physical table tent, sticker or plaque that opens the same mobile menu by NFC tap or QR scan. [TapMe menu destination](https://tapme.contact/destinations/menu)

### Differentiation to test

The strongest hypothesis is that a waiter-led venue will pay for private feedback about a named dish, alongside a separate overall-visit rating, from the menu guests already opened. The public examples show substantial overlap in QR menus, NFC access, general feedback and displaying payment details. The test is whether this combination surfaces issues managers would otherwise miss and whether guests use it during an ordinary visit.
